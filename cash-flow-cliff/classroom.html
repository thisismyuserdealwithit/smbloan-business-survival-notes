Which day does cash run short?

A small business opens the month with A$5,000. A customer owes it A$15,000. Will the business have enough cash on the days its own bills fall due?

This fictional month lasts 30 days. Materials cost A$6,000 on day 7, staff are due A$4,000 on day 14, and another A$2,000 goes out on day 21. Those dates are fixed. The customer pays the entire invoice once. There are no other cash movements.

Build two daily forecasts

In column A the customer pays on day 10. In column B the customer pays on day 25. Start both columns at A$5,000. At each date, add the money received and subtract the money paid. Record each closing balance, including any negative value.

Move the receipt by one day

Keep the original A$5,000 opening cash. Predict the lowest balance if the customer pays on day 14. Predict it again for day 15. Explain what changes on payday. The model nets all movements on the same day before reporting a balance.

Both forecasts first go below zero on day 7. In A, another A$1,000 at the start would bring the lowest balance to zero. In B, the lowest point arrives on day 21 and the extra opening cash needed is A$7,000. The same A$15,000 arrives and A$12,000 leaves in both months, so the closing cash stays A$8,000.

What happens on payday?

With a day 14 receipt, cash starts that day at −A$1,000. The A$15,000 receipt and A$4,000 staff payment leave A$10,000 at close. The lowest daily close remains −A$1,000, first reached on day 7.

With a day 15 receipt, the staff payment comes first. Day 14 closes at −A$5,000. Day 15 closes at A$10,000. Both versions still close the month at A$8,000. Ask which extra fact would matter if payroll had to clear at 9 am on day 14. The answer is the time the receipt clears. The daily model cannot resolve that order.

Use in a class

This is introductory practice for BSBESB403 criteria 2.1 and 2.3, which address working capital and cash-flow projections. For Queensland Accounting, Unit 3, Topic 1, have students reproduce both forecasts in a spreadsheet and change the customer’s payment date. Full assessment requires additional work. This exercise omits GST, financing and profit calculations. Negative balances represent unfunded gaps.

Extension for a second lesson. The business spends A$12,000 over 30 days. That averages A$400 a day. Ask learners why five days of average expenses (A$2,000) would still leave forecast B short. Compare their reasoning with SMB Loan’s expense-buffer method. The worked forecast keeps the exact dates bills are due.
